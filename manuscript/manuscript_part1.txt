1 Introduction
The equivalent-layer technique was introduced during the 1960s and has become a versatile tool for processing and interpreting potential-field data in applied geophysics. It
consists in approximating a discrete set of potential-field data by a linear combination
of harmonic functions representing the potential field due to a discrete set of virtual
sources (e.g., monopoles, dipoles, prisms). These virtual sources are called “equivalent sources” and are commonly disposed on a surface forming a discrete “equivalent
layer”, which is “equivalent” in the sense that it satisfactorily reproduces the observed
potential-field data. The general approach involves estimating the constant physical
property of the equivalent sources (e.g., density, mass, isotropic magnetic susceptibility, magnetization intensity, magnetic moment intensity) by numerically solving a
constrained linear inverse problem. The technique can be used to accomplish various data processing steps, such as interpolation, upward (or downward) continuation,
gradient data processing, merging multiple data sets, estimating density models from
gravity data, computing the amplitude of the anomalous magnetic field, transforming
total-field anomalies into field components, as well as estimating the magnetization
direction and computing the reduction to the pole of magnetic data. Further details
on practical and computational aspects of the equivalent-layer technique can be found
in the recent review by Oliveira Jr. et al. (2023).
Several equivalent-layer methods follow the pioneer approach presented by Dampney (1969) and rely on the numerical solution of the “upward continuation integral”
(e.g., Skeels 1947; Peters 1949; Henderson and Zietz 1949; Dean 1958; Roy 1962; Bhattacharyya 1967; Henderson 1970), which is the solution of the “Dirichlet’s problem” or
the “first boundary value problem of potential theory” on a plane located above the
sources (Kellogg 1967, p. 236). From the physical point of view, this numerical solution gives rise to a predicted potential field analogous to the vertical component of the
gravitational attraction due to a layer of monopoles or the magnetic scalar potential
due to a layer of vertically-magnetized dipoles. This approach provides the theoretical
background for computing the interpolation or upward/downward continuation of any
potential-field data. However, other field transformations that have been successfully
used in practice, such as joint processing of gravity-gradient data (e.g., Barnes and
Lumley 2011) or conversion of gravity disturbance data into the gravity-gradient tensor (e.g., Piauilino et al. 2019; Oliveira Jr. et al. 2023), are empirical generalizations
often assumed to be valid despite the lack of mathematical proof.
Most of the well-known equivalent-layer methods for magnetic data processing are
empirical generalizations of the “Neumann’s problem” or the “second boundary value
problem of potential theory” (Kellogg 1967, p. 246). These methods usually presume,
without any proof, the existence of an equivalent layer of dipoles located above the
sources that can be used to reduce magnetic data to the pole (e.g., Emilia 1973;
Bhattacharyya and Chan 1977; Silva 1986; Leão and Silva 1989; Oliveira Jr. et al. 2013;
Li et al. 2014; Oliveira Jr et al. 2015; Ribeiro-Filho et al. 2020; Reis et al. 2020; Li et al.
2020; Takahashi et al. 2022; Piauilino et al. 2025). Up to now, far too little attention
has been paid to the theoretical aspects related to the existence of a planar equivalent
layer of dipoles or its relationship with a boundary value problem of potential theory
in applied geophysics. By following different approaches in the wavenumber domain,
3

Pedersen (1991) and Li et al. (2014) proved the existence of a planar equivalent layer
of dipoles with all-positive magnetic moment distribution that exactly reproduces the
approximated total-field anomaly (e.g., Blakely 1996, p. 179) caused by sources having
a purely induced magnetization in the vertical direction. Their approach, however, is
valid only for the case in which the total-field anomaly data rely on a plane. Reis
et al. (2020) generalized the works of Pedersen (1991) and Li et al. (2014) to prove
that this planar equivalent layer exists for the case in which the dipoles have the
same arbitrary uniform magnetization direction of the sources, regardless of whether
the sources magnetization is purely induced or not. All those studies assume that the
equivalent layer is defined in a source-free region, where Laplace’s equation is obeyed.
Consequently, all those studies presume that the equivalent layer does not cross the
true sources.
The seminal work of Gunn (1975) was the pioneer in showing a complete description of potential-field transformations in the Fourier domain. That author has also
formulated such transformations in terms of planar equivalent layers of monopoles and
dipoles. However, while presuming the existence of planar layers, Gunn (1975) did not
establish any relationship with boundary-value problems in potential theory and did
not take into account situations in which they intersect the true sources.
There have been no attempts to establish the theoretical background explaining, for
example, the existence of a planar layer of monopoles that simultaneously fit the gravitational potential and its spatial derivatives of first and second orders; the existence
of a planar layer of dipoles that simultaneously fit the magnetic scalar potential and
induction field components due to arbitrarily magnetized sources; or the existence of
equivalent layers that cross the sources. To the authors knowledge, all equivalent-layer
methods found in the literature consider that the layer is located above the sources; a
condition that cannot be ensured in practical situations with field data produced by
anomaly sources having undisclosed depths. The absence of precise depth information
about the true sources, however, does not prevent the real-world application of the
equivalent-layer technique. Existing literature contains numerous examples of its use
with a wide range of real-data applications, from exploration to satellite geophysics
(e.g., Gonzalez et al. 2022).
Here, we present a systematic theoretical discussion on the existence of planar
equivalent layers that reproduce the gravitational and magnetic induction fields due
to 3D sources, even for the case in which the layer crosses them. We limit ourselves to
the case in which the magnetic sources have a total magnetization with constant direction, but spatially-variable intensity. We formulate the existence of continuous planar
layers as the “planar equivalent-layer theorem”, which we mathematically prove by
modifying the solutions of the Dirichlet’s and Neumann’s problems on planar surfaces
that may cross the sources. Our results fill the theoretical gaps in most of the existing equivalent-layer methods, including those presenting alternative formulations (e.g.,
Cordell 1992; Guspı́ and Novara 2009). We also establish how a particular equivalent
layer can be used to perform a particular potential-field transformation. Besides, we
define the theoretical link between potential-field transformations made via equivalentlayer technique and those directly computed from the Fourier transform of the observed
potential-field data.

4

We address theoretical aspects on the existence of planar equivalent layers and their
relationship with potential-field transformations with the purpose of replacing with
the planar equivalent-layer theorem the widely accepted “equivalent-layer principle”.
This principle refers to the empirical generalizations by which most of the studies
found in the geophysical literature justify the existence of equivalent layers allowing
computing potential-field transformations. Rather than a totally new application for
the equivalent-layer technique, the main goal here is to explain or shed new light
on why the technique has long been successfully applied to perform potential-field
transformations. The present work opens the door for future investigations about, for
example, (i) the analysis of discretization and truncation effects applied to continuous
equivalent layers; (ii) the generalization of the reduction to the pole (RTP) to the case
in which the sources have a nonuniform total-magnetization direction; (iii) impact
on potential-field transformations of planar non-null physical-property distributions
that produce no field, that is, the annihilators (Parker 1994, p. 66) or silent sources
(Baratchart et al. 2013, 2021); and also (iv) the relationship between particular planar
equivalent layers and the true 3D sources.
While the focus of this work is on the theoretical foundations of the equivalent-layer
technique, we do not intend to discuss the geophysical implications or the practical
computational implementation of the theoretical framework presented here. To this
purpose, we refer the readers to the companion paper (Oliveira Jr. et al. 2026). In the
companion paper, we discuss how the present theoretical developments shown here can
be used to explain some important theoretical gaps in the equivalent-layer technique.
We also numerically illustrate how different equivalent layers can be used to perform
the same potential field transformations and also show an application to field data.

3 Continuous equivalent layers on a plane
An equivalent layer is loosely defined as a physical property distribution that satisfactorily reproduces potential-field data and enables potential-field transformations.
In the literature, equivalent layers are typically assumed to lie in source-free regions
where Laplace’s equation holds. Consequently, it is presumed that the equivalent layer
does not intersect the true sources. However, in practical applications, the positions
of the true sources in the subsurface are often unknown, making this condition difficult to guarantee. Empirical evidence suggests that equivalent-layer methods can still
perform effectively when the layer intersects the sources (e.g., Gonzalez et al. 2022).
In this section, we formally define continuous equivalent layers on a horizontal
surface S ′′ that may either lie above or intersect the sources (Fig. 1). Our goal is to
leverage the properties of continuous layers to gain deeper insights into their discrete
counterparts, which are explored in the companion paper “Planar equivalent layers
for potential fields: 2. Geophysical implications and applications”.

6 Equivalent layer classes
According to the Planar Equivalent-Layer Theorem (Theorem 1), it is possible to
define different planar equivalent layers pε , r′′ ∈ S ′′ (Fig.1), satisfying the conditions
imposed by Definition 1. Specifically, different equivalent layers pε can be defined on
the same horizontal surface S ′′ , for the same potential field do , by using different
combinations of αd and αa in Eq. 37. The horizontal surface S ′′ may be located above

7 Conclusions
We furnish the theoretical background for potential-field transformations that have
been empirically made in space domain by using equivalent layers since this technique
was introduced in applied geophysics during the 1960s. Our goal here is replacing
the widely accepted equivalent-layer principle, which refers to the empirical generalizations by which most of the studies found in the geophysical literature justify
the existence of equivalent layers allowing computing potential-field transformations.
Specifically, we have formulated the existence of planar and continuous equivalent
layers that reproduce the potential-field data within a predefined error level as the
planar equivalent-layer theorem. We have proven this mathematical statement by
modifying the solutions of the Dirichlet’s and Neumann’s problems on planar surfaces that may cross the sources. We also establish how a particular equivalent layer
can be used to perform a particular potential-field transformation. Besides, we define
the theoretical link between potential-field transformations made via equivalent-layer
technique and those directly computed from the Fourier transform of the observed
potential-field data. In essence, we have filled the theoretical gaps in most of the existing equivalent-layer methods and have also shown that a planar equivalent layer is
not a purely mathematical abstraction, but carries physical meaning and is directly
related to boundary value problems of potential theory. We contribute to the general
understanding on why the technique has long been successfully applied to perform
potential-field transformations. Our work opens the door for future theoretical investigations about potential-field transformations via equivalent-layer technique, as well
as for the relationship between planar equivalent layers and the true sources.
